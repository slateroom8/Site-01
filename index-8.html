Accountability Review

DINR and Air Canada’s Best New Restaurants: A Ranking That Asks for Trust but Offers Few Answers

Ahead of the unveiling of Air Canada’s 2026 Best New Restaurants ranking, questions about its operating partner’s commercial relationships remain unanswered. Air Canada says it found no conflict of interest. It has declined to explain the specific safeguards applied.

By Kyle Williams  ·  October 31, 2026

Air Canada’s Best New Restaurants asks Canadians to trust its judgment. For nominated restaurants, recognition offers national exposure. For readers, the ranking’s value depends on confidence that the selection process is independent.

When asked how that independence is protected from overlapping commercial interests, Air Canada and its operating partner, DINR, provided little readers could examine.

Air Canada said an internal investigation identified no conflict of interest, but declined to explain how the relationships raised were assessed. DINR, asked about its own involvement and business connections, directed the questions back to Air Canada.

The result is a conclusion without a supporting public explanation.

The correspondence did not establish which commercial relationships were disclosed, whether any recusals occurred, or who independently reviewed potential conflicts involving DINR.

Confidentiality may protect scores, deliberations and an anonymous reviewer’s identity. Neither organization explained why it prevented them from describing the general safeguards governing the process.

A commercial platform inside the selection process

DINR is a restaurant reservation and dining platform with commercial interests in the hospitality industry. It also helps operate Air Canada’s Best New Restaurants program.

Air Canada described DINR’s contribution in written correspondence as “editorial guidance, program logistics and industry expertise.” The airline said it retains oversight.

Its public description of the program outlines several stages: a national panel nominates restaurants, an anonymous culinary expert undertakes a dining tour, and the finalists and Top 10 are determined in collaboration with DINR.

DINR therefore occupies a position that deserves scrutiny. It develops business relationships with restaurants while contributing expertise and editorial guidance to a program that recognizes restaurants.

That overlap does not establish preferential treatment. It makes the rules separating commercial activity from selection decisions essential to the program’s credibility.

Yet the precise boundaries of DINR’s influence remain unexplained.

DINR also participates in developing the panel

Air Canada’s first response confirmed DINR’s involvement in developing the 2026 nomination panel.

The airline said Air Canada and DINR reviewed potential conflicts alongside considerations including culinary knowledge, industry credibility and geographic representation. It said Lindsay Brennan was recommended through that process and that Air Canada approved her participation.

That explanation establishes involvement in panel development. It does not explain who proposes candidates, who assesses their relationships, what would disqualify someone, or what restrictions might accompany an appointment.

Approval is a decision. It is not an explanation of the safeguards behind that decision.

Where an operating partner has relationships with restaurants and hospitality businesses, its participation in choosing the nomination panel creates an obvious need for clear boundaries and independent review.

The correspondence did not describe those boundaries.

These are identifiable platform and event relationships. Their underlying payment terms and contracts are not public.

There is an important limit to what current listings establish: Air Canada states that finalists are invited to join DINR. A restaurant’s presence on the platform therefore does not, by itself, prove that it was a DINR partner before nomination or evaluation.

That distinction matters when assessing whether a relationship could have influenced selection. It does not eliminate the need to explain how existing relationships are handled—or how selection activity is separated from recruiting restaurants to the platform.

Does DINR’s commercial team participate in selection discussions? Are evaluators informed of platform relationships? Who assesses potential conflicts involving DINR itself?

Those questions were put to the organizations. They were not specifically answered.

Relationships involving panelists

The overlap extends to restaurants associated with members of the nomination panel.

Lindsay Brennan, a publicly listed 2026 panelist, is associated with Montréal restaurants Alma and Bar Luz. Both appeared in DINR’s reservation directory when checked during this review.

Brennan also operates the wine-importing agency Vin i Vida. Historical wine-offer terms documented a commercial connection involving Vin i Vida and SOMM, the business that acquired DINR. Those terms establish a historical connection; they do not establish the nature of any current arrangement.

Hospitality expertise is a reasonable qualification for a restaurant panel. It also comes with business relationships that require careful assessment.

The relevant question is not whether a restaurateur or wine professional can contribute useful judgment. It is what happens when that person’s commercial relationships intersect with the program, its operating partner or restaurants under consideration.

Air Canada’s published eligibility criteria require nominations without conflicts of interest. But the correspondence did not explain how that requirement is interpreted or enforced.

Questions about disclosures involving Brennan, her restaurants and Vin i Vida—and whether any restrictions or recusals applied—received no specific answer.

An investigation followed by a blanket assurance

Air Canada initially said potential conflicts had been reviewed during panel development and restaurant vetting.

On October 13, it said it was actively investigating the concerns and needed additional time for due diligence. It committed to a formal response by October 20.

The airline met that deadline. Its response said the investigation had identified no conflict of interest and that the program’s principles had been respected.

It then stated that it does not disclose details of its internal selection process and directed the inquiry to the public program page.

Air Canada’s position is clear: it says it found no conflict of interest. What remains unclear is the basis for that finding.

The response did not identify the investigation’s scope, the relationships examined, the standards applied or the safeguards used. Nor did it clarify whether particular relationships were considered outside the definition of a conflict or were addressed through protective measures.

Those distinctions matter. A relationship assessed as irrelevant, a potential conflict managed through recusal, and a conflict overlooked altogether are different situations. The response gives readers no way to determine which assessment was made.

An internal conclusion may satisfy Air Canada. Without an explanation, it offers the public little basis for evaluating the process.

DINR’s answer: ask Air Canada

DINR was separately given the specific questions and an opportunity to respond before publication.

Paul Stasiewich initially requested the writer’s full name, publication and additional context about the article’s scope.

After receiving a reply describing the article, he responded on October 29 that the program is fully owned and overseen by Air Canada. He directed questions about it to the airline.

DINR did not answer the questions about its commercial relationships, involvement in panel development or conflict-management safeguards.

Air Canada’s ownership establishes ultimate responsibility. It does not explain DINR’s conduct or the boundaries of its role.

The two responses leave a gap: Air Canada relies on an investigation it will not explain, while DINR refers questions about its own participation back to Air Canada.

Confidential judging does not explain opaque governance

There are understandable reasons to protect an anonymous reviewer’s identity, unpublished results and individual judging deliberations.

The questions raised here do not require disclosure of any of those things.

The organizations could explain:

  1. What authority DINR holds at each stage of selection.
  2. Which business relationships panelists and evaluators must disclose.
  3. Whether the same person may nominate a restaurant and subsequently evaluate it for the final ranking.
  4. What circumstances require recusal.
  5. How DINR’s relationships with nominees are assessed.
  6. Who reviews potential conflicts involving DINR independently of its commercial interests.

These are basic questions about how a ranking protects its independence.

Neither organization’s response explained why answering them would compromise the judging process.

Recognition requires accountability

The documented relationships do not prove that the ranking was manipulated. The correspondence does not establish that an evaluator favoured a restaurant or that a commercial arrangement determined a result.

It does establish something narrower and significant: specific questions about overlapping business interests were raised, and the responses did not explain how those interests were managed.

Air Canada says its investigation found no conflict. DINR says Air Canada oversees the program. Readers are left with assurances from the organizations whose arrangements are being questioned.

If the program’s independence is adequately protected, explaining those protections should strengthen its credibility.

Until that explanation is provided, the public is being asked to accept the ranking’s independence largely on trust.

This article reflects Air Canada’s and DINR’s responses to the questions raised. It will be updated with substantive clarifications, corrections or additional responses.
